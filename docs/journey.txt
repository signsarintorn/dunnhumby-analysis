# Project journey

A working log of how the analysis developed: what was done, what went wrong, and what changed.
Findings are in the README, details in [eda.md](eda.md) and [methods.md](methods.md).

## Stage 0. Setup and data import

1. Set up folders and drafted the business case before touching the data.
2. The project sat in a OneDrive-synced folder with full storage, so files were cloud-only and
   failed to load. Moved the project off synced folders.
3. Python 3.14 had no ready build of psycopg2, so the loader switched to psycopg 3 and loads with
   PostgreSQL COPY in chunks of 200,000 rows.
4. Briefly considered switching datasets because demographics are anonymised. Kept Dunnhumby,
   since its campaign data supports the promotion question, then dropped and recreated the
   database for a clean load.
5. Row counts matched the source for all 7 tables (2,595,732 transaction rows).
   `causal_data` was skipped (very large, not needed).

## Stage 1. Data quality

1. Found 18,917 rows (0.73%) with zero sales or zero quantity. No negative values, so returns
   are not in the data.
2. First hypotheses were wrong: zero-sales rows were not returns or coupons (coupon discount was 0).
   Store discounts explained 3,748 of them, so these were kept for basket analysis only.
3. Built two views: `transactions_clean` for revenue and customers, `transactions_basket` for
   basket analysis. Excluding rows changed total sales by 19.28, traced to zero-quantity rows.
4. Only 801 of 2,500 households have demographics, and they spend about 3 times more (checked with
   both mean and median). Decided not to use demographics.
5. Hit a ROUND error on double precision values. Fixed with `::numeric`.
6. No duplicate rows found.

Lesson: verify expected results before writing them down.

## Stage 2. EDA

1. Weeks 1 to 16 are a panel ramp-up and were excluded. Week 102 has only 6 days, confirmed by
   checking days covered and sales per day.
2. Weekly stats looked volatile at first (cv 26.5%), but the middle half of weeks varied only 12%.
   The volatility came from the ramp-up weeks.
3. Top and bottom 10 weeks suggested basket value drives sales. Comparing 17-week blocks then
   showed a rise in the last block, which first looked like growth.
4. Flagged fuel and other non-merchandise (about 9% of sales, 668 products). Checked that fuel did
   not explain the late rise in basket value. It did not.
5. Shopping frequency differs about 13 times between light and heavy households, which shaped the
   RFM design.
6. Campaign tables showed overlapping campaigns from week 33, very low coupon redemption (17%),
   and non-random targeting: households later targeted already spent 5.9 times more.
7. Decided to keep EDA descriptive and record hypotheses (H1 to H8) to test later, instead of
   chasing each new result.

## Stage 3. Q1 Customer segmentation

1. Scored households 1 to 5 by quintile cut-offs rather than NTILE, so ties get the same score.
2. Combined Frequency and Monetary into one score after checking they move together
   (91% within one point).
3. RFM "At risk" looked too broad: its median household had been away only 19 days but usually
   shops every 8 to 9 days. Added a rhythm check that compares each household with its own usual gap.
4. A third of "At risk" households turned out to be within their normal rhythm.
5. Tested average vs median gap. Results differed by 17% in households but only about 10% in
   revenue, so both are reported as a range (317 to 372 households).
6. Corrected an addition error in the summary (314 to 317).

## Stage 4. Q2 Campaigns

1. Sales drivers: spend per basket explains 77% of weekly variation (H1 supported).
2. First lift estimate for campaign 18 used a baseline a year earlier and gave about 145,000.
   Decile 1 showed a lift of 31.4 per week, which looked suspicious.
3. A recent baseline (8 weeks before) cut the estimate to about 60,000. The decile 1 lift vanished,
   so those households had reactivated before the campaign.
4. Pull-forward check: no drop after the campaign. Found that targeted households also received
   later campaigns 7 times more often, so the check was repeated without them. Still no drop.
5. Placebo test in weeks with no campaigns: targeted households grew 11.7 per week faster anyway,
   more than the measured lift. Concluded the campaign effect cannot be confirmed with this data.
6. Discounts: coupons are only about 2% of all discounts. Store discounts were out of scope
   (no comparison group).

Lesson: a positive result is not a finding until it survives a placebo test.

## Stage 5. Q3 Baskets

1. Split the rise in spend per basket by commodity. The top items were Christmas goods, turkey,
   baking, soup and cold remedies.
2. Checked seasonality: these items peak exactly 52 weeks apart (turkey weeks 35 and 87,
   Christmas weeks 40 and 92). The late "growth" was the holiday season.
3. This changed the business trigger, the EDA baseline (moved from weeks 34 to 84 to regular
   weeks 50 to 84) and closed H2, H3 and H8.
4. Market basket analysis: high-lift pairs were niche, frequent pairs were loosely linked staples.
   Picked cross-sell candidates in between (lift 4 or more, support 1% or more).
5. Learned that confidence shows overlap, not order or cause, and that a missing item may be
   at home or bought elsewhere.

Lesson: check what is inside a trend before naming it growth.

## Stage 6. Dashboard

1. Built summary tables in SQL so Power BI loads small tables instead of heavy views.
2. Chose the SQL Server connector by mistake at first. Switched to PostgreSQL.
3. Table names loaded as `public dash_...`, so DAX needed quotes.
4. Overview cards used weeks 34 to 84 while the chart labelled those weeks as holiday season.
   Fixed the baseline to regular weeks 50 to 84 and split weeks 17 to 32 into their own label.
5. Cards showed merchandise-only figures (uplift 7.5%) while early notes used all sales (5.7%).
   Aligned the README with the dashboard on merchandise only.
6. Line charts with a period legend drew lines across gaps. Switched to column charts.
7. Small weekly differences were invisible on a 0-based axis, so the driver charts show
   "% vs a regular week" on the same scale.
8. Fixed a refresh error (cyclic reference) by reloading one table, a sort error (circular
   dependency) by building the sort column in Power Query, and a redemption chart that showed
   percent of grand total instead of the actual rate.
